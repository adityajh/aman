# ICP Research — Voice of the Indian Counsellor

*July 2026. Source material, synthesis, and strategy implications.*

---

## 0. Method and its limits — read this before trusting anything below

**What I reached:**

| Source | Type | n |
|---|---|---|
| The Polis Project, *How Mental Health Organizations in India Are Failing Therapists* (May 2025) | Qualitative interviews, Indian therapists | 11 |
| Cureus, *Starting Solo: Early Career Psychiatrists in Private Practice in India* (Jul 2025) | Cross-sectional survey, India | 100 |
| SimplePractice, *2023 State of Therapist Well-Being* | Survey, US practitioners | 550 |
| Indian Journal of Psychological Medicine (2026), Scroll.in, TherapyRoute | Regulatory analysis, India | — |
| Multiple Indian therapy-fee guides (2026) | Market pricing | — |

**What I did not reach:** direct threads from r/therapists, r/psychotherapy, or Indian counsellor WhatsApp and Facebook groups. Searches returned articles *about* practitioner frustration rather than the raw conversations. The Polis interviews are the closest thing to unmediated Indian practitioner voice in this set, and they are drawn from a small, self-selected, urban, largely early-career and progressive-leaning sample.

**Therefore:** the emotional themes below are well-evidenced but the *distribution* is not. Treat this as a map of what exists, not of how common each thing is. The three-person beta is where you verify it.

**One structural bias worth naming:** most available material describes therapists employed by organisations and platforms, not independent solo practitioners. Aman's ICP is the second group. Some pain transfers directly; some doesn't. Where I'm extrapolating, I say so.

## 1. The voice — recurring themes

*All content paraphrased. Direct quotation kept minimal by design.*

### Theme 1 — "I am not being paid what this work costs me"

The clearest and loudest theme. Platform-employed therapists report receiving a fixed ₹500–700 per session while the client is charged ₹1,200–2,500, often without being told the client's fee. Monthly earnings of ₹20,000–30,000 are described even at caseloads of five to eight sessions a day. Pay for therapists with up to five years of experience is reported to cap around ₹4 lakh a year — inadequate for a Tier 1 city.

Independent practitioners earn more, but the reporting is explicit that this depends on referral networks, social capital and visibility rather than on skill.

### Theme 2 — "There is no way to tell if I am any good, and no one to ask"

Good supervisors are described as gatekept through academic networks — if you didn't train at a top institution, you may not know the senior clinicians who could supervise you. Supervision quality is inconsistent where it exists at all.

The emotional consequence is stated plainly in the interviews: one therapist describes leaving an organisation full of doubt, unsure whether she would return to the profession, feeling that every event in the therapy room reflected on her as a person, with no room to learn or make mistakes.

**This is the deepest pain in the material.** It is not administrative. It is not financial. It is *epistemic and it is lonely.*

### Theme 3 — "Clients don't think this is real work"

A recurring line across Indian sources: clients resist paying because they perceive counselling as listening and talking. Fee ladders published for Indian clients are organised almost entirely by *credential* — trainee, independent counsellor, experienced psychotherapist, clinical psychologist, RCI registration, overseas training — with the repeated caveat that cost is not the same as quality.

**The implication is structural:** a counsellor without RCI registration is priced at ₹800–1,500 regardless of how good they are, because the market has no other signal to price on.

### Theme 4 — "Admin is grinding me down, but it isn't the wound"

The US survey data is unambiguous that administration contributes to burnout — a majority of currently burned-out practitioners cite it. But it sits alongside compassion fatigue at a similar rate and low pay just below.

Administration is **the loudest complaint and not the deepest one.** It is the thing people can articulate at a networking event. It is not the thing that makes them leave.

### Theme 5 — "I was made into a salesperson"

Strong theme among platform-employed therapists: pressure to hit client-acquisition targets, upsell packages, produce social media content, and be available beyond hours. One interviewee describes being systematically trained into sales.

For Aman's ICP — independents — this reads as *what they left*, and it defines what they will not tolerate in a tool.

### Theme 6 — **"The metrics were used against me"** ⚠️

The single most important finding in this research, and a direct threat to Aman's core proposition.

A Mumbai therapist at a large mental health startup describes being required to use standardised scales to demonstrate client progress to investors, and says of it: <cite index="84-1">"you internalize failures when the metrics don't reflect success"</cite>. She adds that growth in therapy is not the kind of growth investors want to see.

**A therapist is describing being harmed by outcome measurement.** Not by bad measurement — by measurement imposed from above and read as a verdict on her worth.

Section 5 deals with what this means. It is the most consequential paragraph in this document.

### Theme 7 — exit

75% of one interviewee's graduating cohort is reported to have left their first job within eighteen months; many left the profession. In the US data, roughly a third of burned-out practitioners had considered leaving the field.

## 2. Pains

Ordered by depth, not by volume of complaint.

| # | Pain | Depth | Aman's relationship to it |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **"I don't know if I'm any good, and there's nobody to tell me"** | Deepest | **Directly addressable. This is the product.** |
| 2 | **Isolation** — no peer reference, no benchmark, gatekept supervision | Deep | Addressable via benchmark pool |
| 3 | **Being priced by credential rather than by results** | Deep | Addressable — this is the fee ceiling |
| 4 | Internalising client failure as personal failure | Deep | **Dangerous** — Aman can worsen this. See §5 |
| 5 | Income instability and low absolute income | High | Not addressable. Do not pretend otherwise |
| 6 | Clients not valuing the work | High | Indirectly addressable |
| 7 | Compassion fatigue and burnout | High | Not addressable |
| 8 | Admin drag — invoicing, chasing payment, notes | Moderate | **Addressable. This is the trigger, not the wound** |
| 9 | Clients ghosting without paying | Moderate | Partially addressable |
| 10 | Being made to sell | Moderate | Addressable by *not* doing it |

**The critical distinction: #8 gets them in the door. #1 is why they stay.** A product that solves only #8 competes with free. A product that solves #1 has no competition in India at all.

## 3. Needs

1. **A private mirror.** Somewhere to see their own effectiveness that no employer, investor or platform can access. *Private is doing heavy lifting in that sentence.*
2. **A reference point.** Not a ranking — a sense of whether their experience is normal. Currently supplied only by supervision, which is expensive and socially gatekept.
3. **Permission to be imperfect.** The material describes environments with no room to make mistakes. A tool that surfaces a deteriorating client must read as *information*, never as *indictment*.
4. **Something to show.** For fee conversations, referrer relationships, supervision, and their own reassurance.
5. **Admin that disappears.** Low emotional stakes, high friction. The wedge.
6. **Not to be sold to.** They have been through platforms that turned them into salespeople.

## 5. ⚠️ The finding that should change the product

**The same feature is medicine or poison depending entirely on who holds it.**

The Polis interviews contain a therapist describing standardised outcome scales as a source of shame — because they were mandated by her employer and reported to investors. She internalised the numbers as a verdict on herself.

Aman proposes to give counsellors outcome scores, deterioration flags, and a practice-level effectiveness dashboard. **To a therapist with that history, this is a reproduction of the worst thing that happened to her at work** — unless the framing and the control structure are unmistakably different.

### What separates the two

| Poison | Medicine |
|---|---|
| Imposed by someone else | Chosen by the counsellor |
| Visible to management or investors | Visible only to the counsellor |
| Read as a verdict on worth | Read as information about a client |
| Compared against colleagues | Compared against the client's own baseline |
| Consequences attached | No consequences, ever |
| Framed as performance | Framed as curiosity |

**Aman is currently designed as medicine but is one product decision away from poison.** The decisions that would flip it:

- Selling to organisations or platforms (revenue for them, surveillance for the therapist)
- Any employer- or supervisor-visible view
- Comparative rankings between counsellors
- A public badge or directory
- Framing the dashboard as "your performance"

### Product consequences — implement these

1. **Never sell to organisations. Ever.** Not a "later" market. Selling B2B here poisons the well permanently with the exact ICP being targeted. Put it on the never-build list beside scheduling.
2. **Say the ownership out loud, on the home page.** *Nobody else can see this. Not your employer, not us, not a platform. It's yours, and you can export and delete it.*
3. **Rewrite the flag language.** A deterioration flag must read as *something to look at with this client*, never as *something went wrong*. Include the reason string, never a score of the counsellor.
4. **The practice dashboard needs a framing sentence** above it, permanently: something to the effect that these numbers describe a caseload, not a clinician, and that harder caseloads produce harder numbers.
5. **Do not build streaks, scores, grades or gamification.** A therapist who internalises failure will internalise a broken streak too.

## 6. Willingness to pay

### What the evidence supports

| Item | Evidence | Read |
|---|---|---|
| Software subscription | Indian therapy tools cluster at ₹700–1,500/mo; Zoho gives the admin layer away free | Weak willingness. Admin is a commodity |
| Supervision | UK comparables £100–160 (1:1) and £40–80 (group) per session. **No reliable Indian figures found** | Unknown but real — this is the "am I good?" spend |
| Training, courses, certifications | Heavy activity — CBT, DBT, EMDR, TISS certificates. Explicitly cited as fee-raising | **Strong. They pay for credentials** |

### The inference — and it is an inference

Indian counsellors demonstrably pay for **things that make them better and things that let them charge more.** The entire fee ladder is organised around credentials, which means credentials are the thing the market has trained them to buy.

They do *not* demonstrably pay for admin software, and now cannot be asked to, because Zoho is free.

**Aman Pro is best understood as priced against training and supervision, not against practice-management software.** A ₹1,999/month subscription that tells a counsellor whether they are improving sits in the same mental category as a certification course or a monthly supervision slot — categories where Indian counsellors already spend real money.

**This must be validated, not assumed.** The single highest-value question for the three beta users:

> *"In the last twelve months, what have you spent money on to become a better counsellor — and how much?"*

If the answer is courses and supervision, the pricing holds. If the answer is nothing, ₹1,999 is a fantasy and the whole thesis needs re-testing.

### The fee-ceiling argument, stated carefully

Published Indian fee ladders sort counsellors by credential: independent counsellors ₹800–1,500, experienced psychotherapists ₹1,500–3,000, clinical psychologists ₹2,000–4,000+. A counsellor without RCI registration cannot climb this ladder through skill, because the market has no way to see skill.

Evidence of outcomes is the only alternative signal available to them.

**But per §5, this must never be the hook.** It is what you say to someone at month six who has been measuring and is wondering what to do with it. Leading with it recruits exactly the counsellor whose data cannot be trusted.

## 7. Revisions to the strategy

### 7.1 Confirmed — no change

- **Practice as a cheap, easy wedge.** Admin is a real and loudly-voiced pain, just a shallow one. Correct as a door.
- **Pro as the business.** The deep pain is the "am I any good" question, and Pro is the only thing addressing it.
- **The authenticity-first ICP.** §5 confirms that the wrong user is not just low-value but actively hazardous.
- **No public badges or directory.** §5 upgrades this from ethical preference to product requirement.

### 7.2 Revised

**1. Isolation joins effectiveness as a core pain.**
"I don't know if I'm any good" and "there is nobody to ask" are the same wound. Supervision is gatekept by academic network and cost. The benchmark pool is therefore not just a cold-start fix — **it is a substitute for the peer reference these counsellors cannot otherwise get.** Reframe it in the strategy as a primary feature, not infrastructure.

**2. "Never sell to organisations" moves to the never-build list.**
Currently absent. It is the highest-risk revenue Aman could take: financially attractive, and it would destroy the product's meaning for the ICP.

**3. Add an explicit data-ownership statement to the home page.**
Not a privacy-policy line. A visible promise, above the fold in the trust section, that nobody else can ever see this data.

**4. Reframe the deterioration flag in the copy.**
Currently "you'll know by session three" — good, but it must be paired with language that makes it about the client, not the clinician. A therapist primed to internalise failure will read a flag as an accusation unless told otherwise.

**5. Price Pro against supervision and training, not software.**
Change the anchor. The current comparison is Greenspace plus PractiPal. The better one is *what you already spend to get better at this.* Verify Indian supervision rates before using this publicly — I could not find them.

**6. Add one beta question, and treat it as the most important one.**
*What have you spent money on in the last year to become a better counsellor?* This tests the pricing thesis directly and cheaply.

### 7.3 New risk for the register

| Risk | Severity | Response |
|---|---|---|
| **Aman reproduces the platform trauma it should be curing** — outcome data read as verdict rather than information | **High** | All five measures in §5. Non-negotiable |
| Organisational sales destroy ICP trust | High | Add to never-build list |
| Pricing thesis rests on inferred, unvalidated willingness-to-pay | Medium–High | Beta question above, before any pricing commitment |

> **Every counsellor in India is asked to prove their worth with a certificate. Aman is the only place they can find out the truth for themselves — and nobody else ever sees it.**

The second clause is not a caveat. Based on §5, it is half the value proposition.
